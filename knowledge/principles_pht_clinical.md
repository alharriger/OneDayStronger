# PHT Clinical Principles

Proximal Hamstring Tendinopathy (PHT) is a chronic overuse injury affecting the proximal hamstring tendon at its attachment on the ischial tuberosity. These principles guide safe and effective rehabilitation.

## Load Management

Tendons require adequate load to heal — rest alone is insufficient and prolongs recovery. The goal is to find the optimal loading dose: enough to stimulate tendon remodeling, not so much as to provoke reactive tendinopathy.

Acceptable pain during exercise is ≤ 3/10. Pain above this threshold during a session signals excessive load. Pain that persists more than 24 hours after a session indicates the load was too high and should be reduced.

## Compression Avoidance

Compressive loads on the proximal hamstring tendon are the primary driver of symptom flares and should be avoided, especially in the early and middle stages of rehabilitation.

Movements and positions that create compressive load include:
- Sustained hip flexion beyond 60–70 degrees (seated stretching, forward bends)
- Deep hip flexion under load (squatting to full depth, deadlifting with an aggressive forward lean)
- Seated positions for prolonged periods, particularly on hard surfaces
- Hamstring stretching with the hip flexed — this is contraindicated throughout rehabilitation

As symptoms improve and load tolerance increases, some compressive positions may be gradually reintroduced in later phases.

## Rehabilitation Progression

PHT rehabilitation follows a structured progression through loading types:

1. **Isometric loading** — static contractions at tolerated hip angles. Used to reduce pain sensitization, establish neuromuscular activation, and begin tendon loading without compression. Recommended first-line in high-irritability presentations.

2. **Isotonic eccentric loading** — slow, controlled lengthening contractions. The most important phase for tendon remodeling. Key exercises: Nordic hamstring curl, single-leg Romanian deadlift, prone hip extension with eccentric emphasis. Tempo typically 3–4 seconds on the eccentric phase.

3. **Heavy slow resistance** — full range isotonic loading at slow tempo with progressive load. Builds on eccentric foundation.

4. **Functional and sport-specific loading** — dynamic movements, increased speed and load, activity-specific patterns. Final phase before return to sport or full activity.

Phases should overlap gradually rather than switching abruptly. Rushing progression is the most common reason for setback.

## Phase Duration and Progression Criteria

Each phase should last a minimum of 3–4 weeks before advancement is considered. Tendons adapt slowly — adequate time under the correct load stimulus is essential for structural remodeling.

Progression should be based on symptom response, not just time elapsed:
- Pain during exercise consistently ≤ 2–3/10
- No more than 24-hour post-exercise soreness
- Ability to complete prescribed sets and reps at prescribed load without compensatory movement

Regression triggers (dropping back to a prior phase):
- Pain spiking above threshold for 2 or more consecutive sessions
- Delayed onset soreness extending beyond 24 hours persistently
- Flare of symptoms at rest or with activities of daily living

## Irritability Classification

Irritability level guides initial loading choices:

**High irritability** — significant pain at rest or with minimal provocation, pain > 5/10 during activities of daily living, pain lasting more than a few hours after light activity. Begin with isometrics only at comfortable hip angles. Avoid any loaded hip flexion.

**Moderate irritability** — pain with activity but settles within 1–2 hours, minimal or no resting pain. May begin isometrics and light isotonic work. Monitor 24-hour response carefully.

**Low irritability** — pain only with specific higher-load activities, settles quickly. May progress through eccentric and functional loading more quickly, with appropriate monitoring.

## Workout Modification by Symptom Level

Daily workout type should be selected based on the user's reported pain level at check-in:

- **Pain 0–3/10**: Standard session — follow the phase prescription as written with minor adjustments as clinically appropriate
- **Pain 4–7/10**: Modified session — reduce sets and/or load by 30–50% from the prescription. Avoid any hip-flexion-dominant exercises. Focus on isometric or low-load isotonic work.
- **Pain 8–10/10**: Rest recommendation — do not prescribe exercises. Recommend active rest, ice, and reassessment. High pain levels warrant clinical review.

## Exercises to Avoid or Restrict

Early rehabilitation (Phase 1, high irritability):
- Hamstring stretching in any position
- Seated exercises with hip flexion > 60°
- Running, jumping, or high-load dynamic movements
- Exercises that require sustained forward lean (e.g., bent-over rows, good mornings)

Mid rehabilitation (Phase 2, moderate irritability):
- Deep bilateral squatting patterns
- Full-range hip hinge under heavy load
- Sprint or acceleration work

## Return to Activity Criteria

Before returning to sports or high-load activities:
- Completing Phase 3 or functional phase without symptom flare
- Single-leg strength within 10% of unaffected side (assessed by clinician or estimated from subjective loading tolerance)
- Running mechanics re-established gradually (walk-run progression before continuous running)
- No pain with daily activities of longer duration

## Important Limitations

This application is an educational tool. The principles above represent general guidance for PHT rehabilitation and do not constitute individualized medical advice. Users with neurological symptoms (numbness, tingling, radiating pain, weakness), acute trauma, post-surgical presentations, or symptoms that are worsening despite appropriate management should consult a qualified healthcare professional before continuing a self-directed program.
